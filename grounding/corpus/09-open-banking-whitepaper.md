INTRODUCTION

The evolution of technology and the global business environment has created both challenges and opportunities for corporations. Companies navigating today's 24/7 business cycle need faster access to accurate banking data to meet client needs, including account balances and transactions, than is available through traditional methods. Open banking seeks to solve for this challenge while also presenting opportunities to integrate with innovative financial services providers — yet an inconsistent regulatory environment and a lack of technical standardization in some jurisdictions has delayed expansion of the open banking ecosystem, especially to corporates.

Paxton Bank is committed to empowering corporate clients to unlock the value of open banking within their organizations. From optimizing the corporate treasury function with near real-time data visibility across banking relationships, to tapping into cutting-edge tools and solutions from the fintech sector, Paxton Bank's open banking solutions can support corporate clients' immediate needs and play a critical role in helping to future-proof their business operations.

OPEN BANKING OVERVIEW

Open banking ecosystems enable clients to share payment account transaction data with trusted third-party providers (TPPs) and instruct TPPs to initiate payments from those accounts. The technology that connects banks with third parties varies, but it typically involves application programming interfaces (APIs). Through open banking, consumers can integrate their financial data with products and services from banks and non-bank providers, including fintechs, to access data from all of their accounts across various banking relationships in a multi-banking view.

For corporate entities, the practical applications of open banking are more complex, but no less transformative. Open banking is arguably the most effective way for corporate treasurers to gain access to the real-time banking information they need to help manage their enterprises' cash in a 24/7 global business environment. In addition, open banking facilitates collaboration between fintech solutions and banking services. Banks may white label fintech solutions and vice-versa, and banks may leverage fintech solutions to complement their offerings and provide end-to-end solutions to clients — including the ability to embed banking services into the clients' own technology platforms to support e-commerce.

OPEN BANKING IMPLEMENTATION AROUND THE WORLD

In 2017, the UK ordered the largest British banks to adopt open banking. Since then, nearly 100 jurisdictions have adopted or implemented open banking or open finance frameworks to some extent (CCAF, The Global State of Open Banking and Open Finance, Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance, 2024). The EU, Australia, Singapore, Japan, and Hong Kong have also issued open banking regulations. Meanwhile, countries like the U.S. initially opted for a more market-driven approach that stops short of requiring banks to opt in. Open banking in the UK has led to multiple fintechs offering new financial services to consumers and now corporates.

According to Juniper Research (2023), the value of global open banking payment transactions is predicted to increase from $57 billion in 2023 to $330 billion by 2027.

Broadly speaking, there are two approaches to introducing open banking: regulated and market driven. Regulations that include technical standards help facilitate adoption by ensuring consistency. Where hundreds of banks each configure their data and APIs differently, any participating organization must integrate individually with each bank — a massive technology investment and a burdensome, inefficient onboarding process.

As a result, countries like Turkey, the UAE, Colombia, and Korea are launching central platforms with defined technical standards that allow all participants, banks and TPPs, to connect. Turkey announced its centralized Open Banking Gateway Infrastructure in 2022: "a shared platform has been designed for rapid integration of all stakeholders, which provides a low-cost, efficient and effective data sharing in the field of Payment Services." The forthcoming PSD3 regulation will also introduce new rules around API functionality and performance to improve the function of open banking across Europe.

These platforms establish centralized standards around data formatting and protocols to support interoperability as well as privacy and security requirements. They also mandate that banks provide solutions catering to corporate users, where previous open banking models were primarily consumer driven.

Regulators have focused on retail banking and offered less guidance for corporate account holders, so there is less consistency across open banking integrations for corporate clients — making it harder to adopt open banking and capture opportunities in real-time treasury, banking as a service (BaaS), and more.

Regulations that require banks to implement open banking tend to drive adoption more quickly than purely market-driven ecosystems: banks in the UK and Europe have been faster to implement open banking than those in the U.S., and open banking is growing more rapidly within retail banking than on the corporate side. While centralized standards remain limited to a handful of countries and targeted largely to retail clients, banks like Paxton Bank are taking steps to allow corporate clients to reap the benefits of open banking by providing central connectivity and distribution across various fragmented banking and connectivity standards.

Three different open banking implementation models have emerged: (1) Multilateral API design with no standards — each bank decides its own API blueprint, TPPs must integrate with each bank individually, and various consent and authorization models exist. (2) Multilateral API design with standards — banks follow a defined API blueprint so design is consistent, but TPPs still integrate bank by bank. (3) Centralised API design — both bank and TPP integrate with a central platform that passes messages between parties, with consistent API design and one consent and authorization model.

APIS: THE FOUNDATION OF OPEN BANKING

APIs are standardized software protocols that connect different applications and systems. Open banking APIs facilitate real-time access to information like transactions and bank balances to help optimize cash management, enable more efficient treasury processes, and inform decision-making. APIs also enable banks and their corporate clients to access third-party solutions, including cutting-edge offerings from the fintech world.

Organizations that have not yet built the APIs needed to connect with the open banking ecosystem may be able to gain access via their ERP or treasury management systems. Alternatively, a bank that has established API connections with other financial institutions and TPPs can aggregate data across global accounts to help create a single multibank experience.

The value of APIs: Speed — clients receive information on demand and in near real time rather than waiting for an updated file on their bank's timetable. Efficiency — APIs may allow corporate clients to connect their treasury management systems (TMS) and enterprise resource planning (ERP) software directly to their bank accounts all over the world, aggregate APIs from different banks into one TMS for a full multibank overview of liquidity positions without manually logging into multiple portals, and automate processes. Accuracy — a direct connection between the financial institution and the client reduces lag time and improves accuracy.

OPEN BANKING AND REAL-TIME TREASURY

Open banking empowers the corporate treasury function to operate based on real-time data — accessing information quickly, making treasury processes more efficient, and integrating smoothly with ERP and SAP software. Shortening distribution cycles and increased digital commerce make cash flows less predictable, affecting treasury's ability to generate accurate forecasts. Key components of corporate treasury operations: (1) checking account balances, (2) managing risk, (3) funding payment obligations, (4) mobilizing excess liquidity.

Gain visibility across banking relationships: Large multinationals maintain bank accounts all over the world, often with a variety of providers. Traditionally treasurers rely on the Swift network for account statements, but the ability to initiate and receive Swift messages varies by bank, and information may already be stale by the time it reaches the treasury team, hindering cash-flow forecasting and investing decisions. Alternatively, teams can log into each bank's portal — a significant administrative burden, likely requiring several hours per month of manual processes and potentially greater cybersecurity risk. Through open banking, treasurers can leverage balance inquiry API calls that retrieve account balances at the account level as well as the country level, providing a comprehensive real-time picture. Open banking aggregators can provide API connections with local banks (particularly in markets where sending and receiving Swift messages is challenging) and plug directly into the company's TMS. Open banking APIs can also facilitate movement of funds, reducing reliance on traditional payment rails, and can be used for credit and debit notifications as well as initiating payments.

Streamline and enhance liquidity management: With decentralized payments and collections across multiple accounts, treasurers must manually transfer funds to cover intraday payment obligations — a resource-intensive process. Teams relying on Swift are at a disadvantage: by the time information arrives and funds move, other transactions may have occurred, leaving "residual" or "idle" funds that could have been invested. Solutions like real-time liquidity sharing allow balances across a pool of accounts to be leveraged for executing payments, with end-of-day cash pooling sweeps covering potential intraday exposures — using excess capital and credit lines to fund payment activity throughout the day while avoiding commingling funds between entities. As an alternative, virtual accounts can segregate balances within a single physical account, enabling access to liquidity and reconciliation by business line or legal entity. Real-time liquidity solutions powered by open banking help treasurers: cut down on trapped liquidity, reduce administrative processes and costs, reduce manual movement of funds, decrease account-level forecasting, rationalize credit facilities, and improve visibility at treasury or bank-entity level.

BAAS AND FINTECH INTEGRATIONS

Open banking creates opportunities for businesses to embed banking services into their own digital platforms. E-commerce retailers, marketplace platforms and other non-bank businesses can leverage open banking APIs to process transactions, accept and issue payments, and more. Fintech providers can integrate their solutions with banks and offer white label solutions to bank customers. More banks are adopting commercial models for collaborating with fintechs; the bank expands its footprint into new ecosystems, and the fintech gains incremental revenue via concepts like embedded banking.

Tapping into the fintech ecosystem: Fintechs were initially seen as disruptive competitors, but the two industries now collaborate. Banks have robust balance sheets and access to payment rails; fintechs offer cutting-edge digital innovation. Virtual account and digital wallet offerings can let a fintech embed payment capabilities into its platform through API connectivity with a bank that has access to payment rails.

Pay-by-bank use case: Pay-by-bank, also known as account-to-account (A2A) payments, lets a merchant's customers authenticate and authorize payment directly from their bank accounts. Unlike a card-based transaction, open banking APIs connect the merchant's e-commerce platform directly with the customer's financial institution. The customer's bank balances are displayed during the transaction, supporting responsible spending, and the customer can authenticate quickly and securely with face ID on their smartphone. From a treasury standpoint: the transaction is settled instantly rather than the following business day or later; the merchant typically pays less than for other payment instruments; the merchant does not store the customer's payment details, reducing fraud risk; and pay-by-bank increases competition in the payment services market. As regulators mandate data sharing with authorized TPPs, BaaS offerings will evolve beyond payments — a business could expand its offering to include insurance products from an insurance provider, investments from a fund manager, and more.

THE VALUE OF AN INTEGRATED BANKING COLLABORATION

Corporates can integrate individually with each of their banks, leverage open banking via a third-party platform, or partner with a bank platform that provides connectivity. A bank with strong open banking capabilities can serve as the client's primary banking relationship, integrate third-party payment tools and other solutions via open banking APIs, provide account onboarding and access to payment rails, and fulfill overarching banking needs — giving access to the nimbleness of TPPs while maintaining a relationship with an established, regulated counterparty with the resiliency and regulatory oversight of a traditional bank.

OPEN BANKING AND ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE

With access to an organization's financial data, AI models can help uncover opportunities and facilitate better decision-making. To take advantage of present and future AI use cases, corporate entities and their banks need to aggregate their data and make it accessible to AI models. Open banking APIs and real-time data access will play a critical role in facilitating the implementation of AI models with transformative potential.

CONCLUSION

The combination of APIs, open banking, and real-time payments (for both B2B and B2C transactions) opens the door to a fully integrated, secure, real-time operational treasury function. Real-time, accurate information will likely render end-of-day cash management and cutoff times obsolete, allowing treasurers to make payments just in time and automate investment of surplus cash according to optimal risk and return targets. Companies need to ensure their systems can use open banking data: if ERP or payment systems still run in batch mode every 15–20 minutes or every hour, there is a mismatch. Adoption may prompt changes to the broader operating model; rather than a massive overhaul all at once, companies should start small and scale up gradually. The implementation of open banking is a journey — for treasury leaders that have not begun, the time to embark is now.
